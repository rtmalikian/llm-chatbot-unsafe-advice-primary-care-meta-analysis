# Reviewer report (Gate 2)

Paper: Unsafe medical advice from patient-facing large language model chatbots in primary-care presentations: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Reviewer (persona and bucket): Reviewer 4 — Patient/community reviewer (Bucket E). I am a layperson with no research training. I have used general chatbots such as ChatGPT for health questions for myself and for family members, in a community setting. I am reviewing from the patient's seat only.
Date: 13 November 2026

## Summary (5 to 8 sentences, in your frame)

I came to this paper the way most patients would: I saw "unsafe medical advice", "patient-facing chatbots" and "about 1 in 10", and I wanted to know what that means for the next question I or someone in my family types into a chatbot. The paper is more careful than its headline. It does say, in the abstract, in the plain language summary and again in the conclusions, that no included study graded, one by one, the answers real patients actually received — the main result comes from test question sets graded afterwards by clinicians. That honesty is the strongest thing in the paper for a patient reader, and I want to be clear that it is there, not buried. My worries are about what happens around that honesty: the plain language summary gives "1 in 10" as a firm-sounding number without saying, in the same breath, that the authors themselves rate the certainty as very low, that it does not cover crisis questions, and that it is not my personal risk. The paper also never tells me whose questions these test sets were like — older people, people asking in another language, people who struggle to write a clear question or to read a long answer are simply not described. And the harm that was counted was harm as doctors graded it on paper, not what happened to a real person who read an answer, believed it, and acted on it.

## Overall recommendation
Major revision. Main reason: No Blocker in my area, but several Major points about how a lay reader will understand — and possibly misuse — the headline result. All of them sit in the title, abstract, plain language summary and conclusions, which are exactly the parts a patient will read. Those parts need rewriting and reordering, not just a word changed here and there. I am not commenting on the choice of statistical model or how the pooling was done; that is outside what I can judge.

## Blockers
None in my area.

I looked for one, and I did not find one, for three plain reasons. First, the paper does state the biggest gap openly, in places a patient will actually see (see Major 3 below for the wording problem that remains). Second, the paper gives no advice to any individual patient about their own care, treatment or medicine. Third, the paper does not claim to rank or clear any chatbot product a reader can open today — the plain language summary says so outright. My Majors below are serious, but each one can be fixed in the text; none of them means the study itself cannot be published in some form.

## Majors
1. [Title; Abstract — Authors' conclusions; Plain language summary] The headline "1 in 10 unsafe" will be read as a personal risk, and the parts that stop that reading come too late. The title says "patient-facing" chatbots and says nothing about test questions. A patient who has just been told by a chatbot to wait and see will read "about one response in ten was rated unsafe" and think "so there was a 1 in 10 chance the answer I got was unsafe." That is not what was measured. The answers were answers to study question sets, given between 2023 and 2026, on older and newer models mixed together. The plain language summary does carry the saving sentence — "They are not a safety rating for any chatbot product a reader can open today" — but it is the very last line, after the number has already landed. What would fix it: put the limit next to the number, everywhere the number appears for a lay reader. For example, in plain words in the plain language summary: "1 in 10 of the answers to test questions were graded unsafe. This is not the chance that the answer you received was unsafe." Consider adding words such as "in test question sets" to the title or subtitle, so the title on its own does not promise more than the study did.
2. [Plain language summary; Summary of findings table] The plain language summary presents "about 1 in 10" without the authors' own warning that the certainty is very low. The abstract and the summary of findings table say the certainty was "very low" for every pooled outcome. The plain language summary — the one part written for people like me, and the part most likely to be read on its own, copied, or quoted — never says it. It also never says, in plain words, how wide the spread was: some sets had no unsafe answers, one set had about 3 in 10, and the paper's own prediction interval runs from about 4 in 100 to about 26 in 100. A lay reader is left with a tidy number and no sense that the authors themselves trust it only weakly. What would fix it: add two or three plain sentences to the plain language summary, next to the 1 in 10 figure: that the authors rate their certainty as very low, what "very low" means in ordinary words ("the true figure could be quite different"), and that the result varied a lot from one question set to another.
3. [Plain language summary, paragraph 3; Abstract — Main results and Authors' conclusions; Results — Primary outcome: human-user stratum] The "no real patients" point is stated — but the wording can mislead in the opposite direction, and what real-person studies did find is missing where patients will look. Credit first: the abstract says "The primary pool was bench-only, since no human-user study reported response-level safety ratings", the conclusions say "no included study rated the safety of answers that real patients received", and the plain language summary says it in the clearest words in the paper. That is visible, not buried, and it passes the basic honesty test. The problem is the next step. The plain language summary says "No study followed real patients using a chatbot" — a lay reader will take that as "nobody has ever studied real people using these things." The Results section shows that is not quite it: real people were studied (for example, a study that randomised members of the public, and a standardised-patient study), but those studies counted the decisions people reached, not a safety grade for each answer. And some of what they found matters greatly to patients — the narrative set reports that people often followed chatbot advice even when the study team judged it wrong, and one human-user study reports inappropriate medication recommendations in a large share of encounters. None of that reaches the abstract, plain language summary or conclusions, so a patient is told the cupboard is bare when it is actually stocked with a different, worrying kind of evidence. What would fix it: in the plain language summary and conclusions, separate the two statements in plain words: (a) no study graded the actual answers real patients received, one by one; and (b) studies of real users do exist, they looked at what people decided and did, and here in one sentence is the main thing they found.
4. [Plain language summary; Abstract — Selection criteria; Conclusions] The plain language summary never says that the scariest questions are outside the main count. Mental-health crisis and self-harm question sets were excluded from the pools — the abstract's selection criteria say so, for a reader who gets that far and understands it, but the plain language summary does not mention it at all. A parent who has typed a crisis question into a chatbot at 2 a.m. is one of the readers most likely to pick this paper up, and nothing in the lay summary tells them "this number does not cover questions like yours." The same applies, in a smaller way, to being told to stay home when you should go to emergency care: the paper reports that problem (undertriage) study by study, with high rates in small emergency sets, but the plain language summary only mentions the emergency set under the general unsafe grade. What would fix it: add a plain sentence to the plain language summary naming what the 1 in 10 figure does not cover — crisis and self-harm questions were kept out of the main count — and one plain sentence saying that in small emergency studies, chatbots quite often advised a lower level of care than they should have, which is why emergency symptoms are a special case in this evidence.
5. [Methods — Criteria for considering studies; Results — Description of studies; Discussion — Completeness and applicability] I cannot tell whether people like my family are in this evidence at all, and the paper never discusses it. The paper tells me most question sets were written by the researchers themselves or taken from websites, and that most sets had no record of how the questions were chosen. It never tells me, in any section: what language the questions were asked in, overall; whether the questions sound like an older person's questions; whether any set used the short, messy, misspelled, half-finished way real people — especially people with low health literacy, people typing in a second language, or people in pain or frightened — actually ask; or how anyone with poor eyesight, shaky hands or no confidence with phones is represented. One study was bilingual and one used Japanese vignettes, so other languages are not wholly absent — but the paper gives a patient reader no way to know whether the pooled result rests mostly on clean, well-written English questions. A well-written question probably gets a better answer than the one my relative would type. What would fix it: a short, plain paragraph in Completeness and applicability naming who is missing or undescribed — non-English questions, older users, people with low health literacy or low digital confidence — and saying honestly that the 1 in 10 figure may not carry over to them. No new pooling is being asked for; an honest paragraph about the gap is enough from a patient reviewer.
6. [Objectives; Methods — Outcome measures; Discussion — Summary of main results] The outcome that was counted is the outcome as doctors see it, and no patient was in the room when "unsafe" was defined. Every safety grade in the main pool was given by clinician raters, using scales each study invented. I understand why doctors grade medical safety. But harm to a patient is wider than a wrong clinical grade: an answer can be clinically "acceptable" and still leave a person terrified, confused, unable to afford the thing it suggests, or unable to tell what to do next — and an answer can be graded unsafe without the reader ever acting on it. The paper does not say, anywhere I could find, whether any patient or member of the public helped choose the questions, define what counts as harm, or read the plain language summary. That is a gap in a paper whose whole subject is advice given to patients. What would fix it: state plainly, in the methods or declarations, whether there was any patient or public involvement; if there was none, say so as a named limitation in the discussion, in words that admit the counted harm is clinician-graded harm on paper, not harm as patients experienced it.

## Minors
1. [Plain language summary, paragraph 2; Abstract — Main results] The counts do not line up in a way a lay reader can follow. The plain language summary says "Across 23 studies", the abstract says 24 studies are in the primary pool, and only the Results explain that one study (Ariel 2026) printed no per-question counts, so it could not go into the pooled number. A careful lay reader will spot 23 versus 24 and wonder which is wrong. Fix: one clause in the plain language summary — "a 24th study could not be counted because it did not publish its numbers question by question" — or wording to that effect.
2. [Abstract; Plain language summary] Words a lay reader has to guess at appear in the parts meant for them. "Bench-only", "response-level", "stratum" and "prediction interval" are used in the abstract without a plain explanation. The plain language summary mostly avoids them, to its credit, but it still rests on "graded answers" without ever saying the graders could see which chatbot wrote each answer in some studies — a fairness point a lay reader grasps at once ("the marker knew whose work it was") and that the risk of bias section raises in technical language only. Fix: swap in ordinary words in the abstract and plain language summary, or add a half-sentence explanation at first use.
3. [Narrative set, final paragraph] One sentence about real users reads as blaming the user. The paper says people "often follow chatbot advice, including advice the study team judged wrong, while rating their own confidence highly." Read from the patient's side, that sounds like the fault sits with the person. The missing half is in the paper's own background: chatbot answers "arrive in a confident register". People believe confident answers partly because the product sounds sure of itself. Fix: reword so the burden sits where it belongs — for example, that answers are written in a confident style, and studies found people tended to follow them, including wrong ones.
4. [Plain language summary; Conclusions — Implications for practice] There is nothing for the reader who is now worried about an answer they already acted on. The paper is right not to give anyone individual medical advice, and I am not asking it to. But a patient who reads "1 in 10 unsafe" about a tool they used last week is left with a worry and nowhere to take it. Practice implications are written for "a clinician or service lead" only. Fix: consider one general, non-personal signposting sentence in the plain language summary or practice section — along the lines that anyone worried about an answer they have already followed should check it with their own doctor, pharmacist or local urgent service, rather than judge it from this paper. That is general information, not advice to an individual.

## Nits
1. [Plain language summary, first sentence; Abstract — Background] "Instead of asking a doctor first" and "questions they once brought to primary care" quietly assume the reader had a doctor they could ask. Many people use a chatbot because they could not get an appointment, could not afford a visit, or it was the middle of the night — the background section itself mentions cost and hour. A small wording change ("when they cannot, or before they can, ask a doctor") would sound less like the reader made a careless choice.
2. [Title] "Primary-care presentations" is professional language. Ordinary readers say "the kind of problems you would take to your GP or family doctor" — which is how the plain language summary does put it, well. No change needed in the technical title if the journal requires that form, but the lay summary's version should be the one used in any press or social wording.
3. [Methods — Types of studies] The word "laypeople" is used for people like me. "Members of the public" or "patients" reads more respectfully and costs nothing.

## Numbers checked
I checked the lay-facing numbers against the results sections and tables in the manuscript itself. I did not check any number against the dataset, and I have no view on how any pooled number was calculated.

- "About 1 in 10" (plain language summary and conclusions) against the pooled unsafe proportion of 10.5%, from 552 of 4,868 graded responses across 95 model arms from 23 studies (Abstract; Results — Primary outcome; Table 1): the rounding to "1 in 10" is fair.
- "Some question sets produced no unsafe answers at all" (plain language summary) against the two sets reported at 0% (Wen 2026; Bilgic 2026): matches.
- "An emergency triage set produced the highest share, about 3 in 10" (plain language summary) against Buis 2023 at 31.1% (23 of 74): matches, although one other set (Liang 2026, herpes zoster, 28.7%) is nearly as high and is not mentioned in the lay summary — not an error, but "highest" rests on a small gap.
- "552 of 4,868" and the crude share of 11.3% (Results) against the pooled 10.5%: both figures appear in the paper. A lay reader will not know why the simple fraction and the headline figure differ, and neither the abstract nor the plain language summary explains it in words. I raise this as a clarity point only, not as a comment on which figure is right.
- Certainty: "very low" for every pooled outcome appears in the abstract, the summary of findings table and the discussion — and, as noted in Major 2, nowhere in the plain language summary.
- Human-user figures I cite (Bean 2026; Si 2026, including the 336 of 496 encounters figure) are taken from Results — Primary outcome: human-user stratum, as printed there.

## Closing paragraph
If this paper is published as it stands, the worst true thing a critical reader could say about it, from a patient's point of view, is this: you counted how doctors marked chatbot answers to exam-style questions, and then you announced the result to the public under a "patient-facing" headline — while no patient was in the room at any point. No patient's real answer was graded. No patient helped decide what "unsafe" means. And the paper cannot even tell us whether the questions resembled the ones asked by an older person, a frightened parent, or someone writing in their second language with one thumb on a phone. So the frightened reader who takes "1 in 10" home with them is carrying a number that was never measured on anyone like them, in any situation like theirs — and the paper's own honest sentences admitting exactly that are placed just far enough from the number that the number is what will be remembered, quoted, and worried over.
