# Reviewer report (Gate 2)

Paper: Unsafe medical advice from patient-facing large language model chatbots in primary-care presentations: a systematic review and meta-analysis (Review 4)
Reviewer (persona and bucket): Reviewer 1 — Bucket B. Evidence synthesis methodologist, 16 years in a Cochrane-style reviews unit, academic setting. Stance: protocol discipline first. Calibrated bias: over-values protocol purity. Remit: PROTOCOL.md domains 2.1, 2.2, 2.3, 2.6, and the GRADE/reporting parts of 2.8. Must not rule on: statistical estimator detail (not ruled on anywhere below).
Date: 2026-11-13

## Summary (5 to 8 sentences, in your frame)

This review asks a clean question — what proportion of responses from patient-facing LLM chatbots fall in a study's explicit unsafe, harmful or dangerous tier, for primary-care presentations — and its architecture is unusually disciplined: a protocol and PROSPERO-ready form fixed before screening, a go/no-go gate passed exactly at threshold with its caveats recorded, dual screening with adjudication, dual extraction with full settlement, strata never pooled together, an empty human-user stratum reported as a finding rather than filled, and an undertriage pool whose pooled mean the authors themselves disown. The headline arithmetic traces exactly to the settled files (see Numbers checked). The problem is that the discipline is not uniform. The primary pool's central selling point — that it counts only explicit unsafe-tier responses — is not what the settled extraction labels show: most pooled events come from tiers labelled "potentially unsafe", "potentially harmful" or "major safety error". The model-era subgroup as analysed is not the subgroup the protocol pre-specified, yet it is presented as pre-specified, with no amendment filed under the protocol's own amendment rule. And there is no consolidated deviations account: the pilot extraction the extraction form itself prescribes was not run and is described only as a departure from "the usual order", coordinator scope rulings that moved studies into the pool are not reported, and a pre-specified red-flag subgroup is dismissed on a statement that the review's own extraction notes contradict. None of this is fatal to the review's existence; all of it is fatal to its current claims about itself.

## Overall recommendation
Major revision. Main reason: two Blockers — the primary outcome as pooled does not match the primary outcome as protocolled and as claimed, and a pre-specified subgroup was redefined without an amendment yet is reported as pre-specified. Both are fixable (relabel and re-analyse, or correct the claims), but neither can be waved through, and several Majors sit behind them.

## Blockers
1. [Abstract; Background paragraphs 3–4; Plain language summary; Methods: Outcome measures; Table 1] The primary pool does not implement the primary outcome definition as written, and the manuscript claims it does. The protocol (stage0-protocol/protocol.md, Outcomes) and the manuscript both define the numerator as responses placed in a tier the study explicitly labels unsafe, harmful or dangerous; the Background claims this line "buys a pooled number that means the same thing in every contributing study", and the plain language summary says only a study's "worst safety grade, the grade the study labelled unsafe, harmful or dangerous" was counted. The settled extraction sheet's verbatim tier labels (extraction-settled.csv, field 15, collected precisely so this could be audited) show otherwise. Coding the 95 pooled arms by their study's label: 26 arms (191 of 552 events, 34.6%) carry a strict unsafe/harm label; 55 arms (327 events, 59.2%) carry potentially-inclusive labels ("potentially unsafe", "potentially harmful", "unsafe or potentially unsafe", "potentially unsafe or misleading", "potential harm"); 14 arms (34 events, 6.2%) carry error labels ("major safety error", "major clinical or safety error", "critical error"). A "potentially unsafe" tier is not a direct equivalent of an unsafe tier — it is, on the manuscript's own logic ("Everything short of that tier is a secondary outcome"), short of it, and combined tiers such as "unsafe or potentially unsafe" merge the primary tier with the tier below it. This is the paper's headline number and its stated differentiation from the competing record CRD420261347066 (a single harm denominator), so the gap between claim and pool is submission-blocking, not presentational. The manuscript also gives the reader no way to check: Table 1 lists topic, arms, counts and risk of bias but not the tier label each study contributed. What would fix it: (a) add the verbatim tier label for every primary study to Table 1 (the data already exist in the settled sheet); (b) either re-analyse the primary pool restricted to strict explicit-tier studies and present that as the primary analysis, or relabel the outcome honestly throughout (title claims, abstract, plain language summary, Background, Conclusions) as the studies' unsafe-or-potentially-unsafe tiers as defined by each study, and delete the "means the same thing in every contributing study" claim; (c) add a sensitivity analysis by label strictness (strict vs potentially-inclusive vs error labels). Any of these routes is acceptable; the current wording is not.
2. [Objectives, item 3; Results: Subgroup analyses; Table 2; Abstract] The model-era subgroup as analysed is not the subgroup pre-specified in the protocol, it is presented as pre-specified, and no amendment was filed. The protocol (stage0-protocol/protocol.md, Subgroups; also concept-and-protocol.md and prospero-form.md field 26) pre-specifies "GPT-3.5 era versus GPT-4, GPT-4o era and later". The extraction form (field 12) silently re-operationalised era as a calendar rule for all model families — release before 13 May 2024 = early, on or after = late — and the manuscript's Objectives then list the calendar rule as the third "pre-specified subgroup contrast". The two rules classify the evidence differently in the direction that matters: of the 3 early arms that drive the interaction (p = 0.001, reported in the abstract), two are GPT-4 arms (Armbruster 2024 GPT-4, 16/100; Buis 2023 GPT-4, 8/37), which the protocol's wording places in the later-era group. The protocol's Amendments section requires any change to subgroups after 2026-11-12 to be filed as a dated amendment before the affected analysis and logged in llm-team-log.md; no amendment appears in the log or the manuscript, and the manuscript contains no deviations section at all. A significant subgroup result (24.3% vs 10.2%) is therefore carrying a confirmatory label it has not earned. The authors' own caution that the contrast is confounded is noted and welcome, but it does not cure the pre-specification claim. What would fix it: either re-run the contrast under the protocol's GPT-era rule and report both, or keep the calendar analysis but relabel it everywhere (Objectives, Results, Table 2, abstract) as a post-protocol operationalisation, file the dated amendment in llm-team-log.md now and disclose in the manuscript that it is retrospective, and downgrade the language from "pre-specified subgroup" to exploratory for this contrast.

## Majors
1. [Abstract: Main results; Summary of findings; Results: Secondary outcomes] Poolability is judged inconsistently across outcomes. For undertriage the authors correctly refuse their pooled mean (prediction interval 0.1 to 96.1%) and report study by study — exactly right, and then still print the disowned GLMM mean (14.6%, 11.9 to 17.8) in the Results text. For the problematic tier they do the opposite: a pool with I² 96.6%, a prediction interval of 2.8 to 64.8%, and tier definitions the text itself says change meaning between studies ("an intermediate accuracy grade in one, a combined error tier in another") is pooled, headlined in the abstract as 18.7% (17.2 to 20.2) with no prediction interval, and carried in the Summary of findings as a quantitative result whose narrow confidence interval conveys a precision the prediction interval refutes. The sentence "its honest reading is that intermediate-tier problems appear about twice as often as explicit unsafe ratings" is a pooled claim built on the pool just declared to summarise unlike measures. What would fix it: apply one poolability standard, stated in Methods and applied per outcome — either demote the problematic pool to the same study-by-study treatment as undertriage (pooled figure, if kept at all, in the analysis supplement only), or keep it pooled but report the prediction interval wherever the pooled value appears, including the abstract and Summary of findings, and remove the "about twice as often" ratio claim.
2. [Methods overall; prisma-checklists.md item 24] There is no consolidated deviations-from-protocol account, the departures that are named are understated, and the PRISMA checklist's claim that "Departures named in the text: no pilot extraction phase; OpenAlex route partial" is therefore incomplete. Three specifics. (a) Pilot extraction: the extraction form (stage3-extraction/extraction-form.md, "Pilot" section) prescribes that "the first five included studies are extracted by both extractors as the pilot", and the protocol describes extraction as onto "the piloted form". No pilot was run. The manuscript (Methods: Data extraction; Discussion: Potential biases) discloses the fact but frames it only as departing from "the usual order" — a reader cannot tell this was a departure from this review's own pre-specified instrument, not from generic custom. (b) Coordinator scope rulings: scope-rulings.md records six rulings at full-text merge. The three exclusions are visible in the flow ("3 more under coordinator scope rulings"), but the inclusions are nowhere in the manuscript: diabetic kidney disease (R3023) ruled IN, superseding a gate-stage scope NO, and cancer survivorship (R2120) ruled IN on a borderline flag — both are in the primary pool (Table 1) with no trace of the ruling. The gate-stage adjudication that counted late-life depression in scope on the mental-health boundary (gate-report.md, caveat 2), including its condition that crisis-specific items be excluded at extraction if separable, is likewise unreported; the manuscript never states whether any items were so excluded, and a second mental-health-adjacent primary study (Xiao 2026a, psychological support for depression and anxiety) enters with no ruling mentioned at all, against a Methods sentence that says mental-health sets "were excluded from the pools". (c) Registration: the title page honestly states no PROSPERO number has been issued, but neither manuscript nor checklist states the material fact that the PROSPERO-ready form, although completed before screening, was never submitted — prospective registration is no longer obtainable for a completed review, and a journal reader should not have to infer that. What would fix it: add a short "Deviations from the protocol" subsection listing every departure (no pilot against the form's own pilot plan; the era operationalisation under Blocker 2; scope rulings in both directions with record IDs and the counts recorded in scope-rulings.md; the mental-health boundary adjudication and whether crisis items were separable; the OpenAlex partial route and the unretrieved 138, already well disclosed; the 4 author-flagged records, already well disclosed; forward chasing not run; registration prepared but not submitted), and correct PRISMA item 24 to match.
3. [Methods: Certainty of evidence; Summary of findings; Discussion: Quality of the evidence] The GRADE application does not fully honour the four pressure points the framework named before results were seen (grade-framework.md P1–P4), and the manuscript's one-sentence GRADE summary hides the two places where it bends. (a) Primary outcome, inconsistency (P3): the framework says inconsistency "presses where intervals span both trivial and serious harm rates"; the primary prediction interval is 3.8 to 26.2%, which does exactly that, yet grade-assessment.md records no inconsistency downgrade, resting partly on subgroup/descriptive contrasts (a confounded 3-arm era contrast and a one-study emergency contrast) that the manuscript itself declines to treat as findings. Either downgrade, or give a fuller justification that does not lean on those contrasts. (b) Problematic outcome, definition heterogeneity (P1): the assessment folds indirectness "inside the inconsistency downgrade to avoid double counting", so the pre-named central pressure point never appears as its own judgement for the outcome where it bites hardest; the Summary of findings comment ("tier definitions differ") is left to do the work of a GRADE domain. Rate P1 explicitly per outcome, even if the conclusion is no additional downgrade, with the double-counting reasoning shown. (c) The manuscript text reports only "downgrades for risk of bias and indirectness" for the primary outcome; a reader of the paper alone cannot reconstruct any of the per-domain judgements that grade-assessment.md contains. Carry a compact per-outcome GRADE table (domain, judgement, one-line reason) into the manuscript or its supplement and reference it. (I express no view on the estimator or on starting proportion evidence at Low, which was the series' pre-set convention; the flags are about applying the review's own pre-named pressure points transparently.)
4. [Results: Subgroup analyses] The disposition of the pre-specified red-flag subgroup rests on a statement the review's own extraction contradicts. The manuscript states: "No primary study prints unsafe counts split that way (Xiao 2026b splits undertriage counts, not unsafe counts), so no interaction test was fitted." The settled extraction for Xiao 2026b (R0234) codes red_flag_split = yes and its notes record, from the paper's Table 5B, high-risk-stratum counts for the study's unsafe tier ("major safety error"): ChatGPT 4/30, Gemini 6/30, Doubao 10/30, within prespecified low-, moderate- and high-risk strata of 30 questions each. Either the extraction notes or the manuscript sentence is wrong. What would fix it: re-check R0234's full text; if risk-stratified unsafe-tier counts exist for all strata, report the within-study descriptive contrast the protocol envisaged (interaction testing is the statistician's call, not mine); if only the high-risk stratum is printed, correct the manuscript sentence to say so precisely. As written, a pre-specified subgroup has been closed on an inaccurate premise.
5. [Methods: Search methods; Results: Description of studies] The citation-chasing yield (80 of 166 included studies) passed through an un-pre-specified single-rule exclusion that the protocol's screening principles disallow elsewhere. Of 968 chasing candidates, 269 (28%) were removed by a logged title triage because their titles "name no LLM, chatbot or AI technology", before dual screening; the protocol's screening section states that a coded rule "may triage; it never finally excludes". The triage is disclosed in the manuscript, which is to the authors' credit, but it is not in the protocol, not justified (a study of chatbot advice whose title names a product or a clinical topic but no technology word would fail it), and no check on the excluded 269 is reported. The manuscript then uses the chasing result — "no primary study" among 80 — to suggest "the primary pool is close to complete", a claim the triage weakens. What would fix it: dual-screen the 269 title-triaged records, or a random sample of them with the miss rate reported, and either soften the completeness suggestion or rest it on the checked result.

## Minors
1. [Table 2; Results: Subgroup analyses] The era rows silently omit 4 of 95 arms: era is "not_reported" in the analysis dataset for Wang Y 2026 (3 arms, recurrent aphthous stomatitis) and An 2026 (1 arm), so 3 + 88 = 91, not 95. State the unclassified arms and their counts wherever the era contrast is tabled.
2. [Abstract: Data collection and analysis; Methods: Selection of studies] The manuscript says "one adjudicator read and settled every disagreement" (screening). llm-team-log.md records that the 628 disagreements "were read by adjudicators (three batches)". Reconcile the two accounts — number of adjudicators is a methods fact, and this review's credibility rests on its process reporting being exact.
3. [Methods: Criteria for considering studies for this review] The protocol's publication eligibility window (primary studies published or posted from 1 January 2023 to the search date) is never stated in the manuscript's eligibility criteria, which give only the model-evaluation years (2023 to 2026). Add the publication window and the preprint eligibility statement.
4. [Results: Subgroup analyses] The Buis 2023 contrast (31.1% vs 11.0% crude for the other 22 studies) sits under the "Subgroup analyses" heading. The analysis output's own wording — "a between-study contrast of one study against the rest and is not a subgroup finding" — is the correct caveat and is stronger than the manuscript's "rests on one study". Carry that sentence, or equivalent, into the manuscript so the contrast cannot be quoted as a subgroup result.
5. [Results: Primary outcome; Discussion: Potential biases] Derived counts deserve one quantified sentence. From the analysis dataset's derived flag: 34 of 95 primary arms are derived-count arms, carrying 246 of 552 events and 2,247 of 4,868 responses (46.2% of the primary denominator). The derivation rules were pre-specified in the extraction form, every derived count is flagged, and the excluding-derived sensitivity (10.0% on 61 arms) is reported — the practice is sound; only its scale is invisible to a manuscript reader. State the share in Results and in the limitations.
6. [Objectives, item 4; Summary of findings] The human-user decision outcomes (Bean 2026; Si 2026) are an objective of the review and are reported narratively, but no certainty judgement attaches to them anywhere in the manuscript: the GRADE framework anticipated rating human-user evidence within its stratum, and grade-assessment.md offers "Low at best" for the Si 2026 direction in the worksheet only. Either add a Summary-of-findings row with a certainty statement for the decision outcomes, or state explicitly in the manuscript why no rating is offered.

## Nits
1. [Table 1, Draelos 2026 row] "70/666 counted; Gemini arm 222 rated, count in figure only" is accurate but easy to misread as a denominator error against the study's 888 responses. A footnote giving the study total (888 = 666 counted + 222 figure-only) would close it.
2. [Title page; Methods: Search methods] Search date and version date are both 12 November 2026, and the manuscript promises the competitor-registry check "will be repeated on any day this review is submitted". Make sure that promise survives into the submission checklist; a stale "no publication located" claim is the kind of detail a journal reviewer will test first.

## Closing paragraph
If this paper is published as it stands, what is the worst true thing a critical reader could say about it? That its one claim to methodological distinction — a strict, pre-specified, single harm denominator that separates it from the broader competing review — is not true of its own pool: nearly two-thirds of the events behind the headline "one in ten responses is unsafe" come from tiers their studies labelled only potentially unsafe, potentially harmful or major error, a redefinition of a pre-specified subgroup was absorbed without the amendment the authors' own protocol requires, and the departures the paper does disclose are the ones that cost it least. The arithmetic is honest and the empty human-user stratum is reported with rare candour, which is precisely why the discrepancy matters: a reader who trusts this paper's process reporting — and the paper asks, on every page, to be trusted on process — would repeat a purity claim the review's own extraction sheet refutes.
