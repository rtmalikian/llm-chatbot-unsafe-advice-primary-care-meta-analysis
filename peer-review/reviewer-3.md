# Reviewer report (Gate 2)

Paper: Unsafe medical advice from patient-facing large language model chatbots in primary-care presentations: a systematic review and meta-analysis (Review 4)
Reviewer (persona and bucket): Reviewer 3 — Bucket A. Primary care physician and former small-practice operator, 18 years community practice.
Date: 2026-11-13

## Summary (5 to 8 sentences, in your frame)

I read this the way I would read anything that might change what happens in my consulting room: are these the questions my patients actually bring, in the order and mess they bring them, and does the number at the end describe that world? On that test the paper is careful in its machinery and much less careful in its framing. The pool it calls "primary-care presentations" is, by its own Table 1, overwhelmingly single-condition question sets — chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, bacterial vaginosis, herpes zoster, mpox, diabetic kidney disease, traumatic brain injury, and similar — most of them post-diagnosis information or self-management questions rather than the undifferentiated first presentation a general practitioner fields, plus one emergency-department triage set whose "unsafe" label means something clinically different from every other study's. The one genuinely recognisable mixed primary-care set (Draelos 2026) is also the largest, and the paper deserves credit for keeping the human-user stratum honestly empty, for refusing to summarise undertriage by its pooled mean, and for stating plainly that its numbers rank no current product. But the title, abstract and Implications for practice repeatedly let a reader take away more than that: a "base rate" for local service decisions, and a claim about emergency material that rests on one study. A practising clinician reading only the abstract would not learn that 19 of the 24 primary studies are China- or Hong Kong-based question sets, that disposition thresholds are therefore system-specific, or that the highest "unsafe" share in the pool is a triage-disagreement rate from an emergency department. None of this is fatal, and none of it needs the review rebuilt; it needs the framing narrowed to the pool that was actually assembled, one sensitivity the paper conspicuously lacks, and the applicability facts put where a clinician will see them.

## Overall recommendation
Major revision. Main reason: no Blocker and no Rule 10 clinical-advice passage was found, but the title/abstract framing of the pool as "primary-care presentations", the undisclosed definitional status of the Buis 2023 emergency-triage set inside the primary pool, and an Implications for practice section that converts a very-low-certainty bench estimate into a "base rate" for local decisions are each Majors that would mislead a clinical reader if published unfixed; one of them (Buis) requires a new sensitivity analysis, so this is not a text-only loop.

## Blockers
None. I scanned the full text specifically for Rule 10 items within my remit — any passage readable as individual clinical advice, a medication recommendation, or advice to clinicians or patients about using a chatbot for their own care (Background, Plain language summary, Discussion, Conclusions / Implications for practice, Declarations). No such passage was found: the manuscript makes no recommendation about any chatbot product, gives no dosing or treatment instruction, and its only directive sentence to service leads is conditional on local evaluation. The problems below are over-claiming and framing (Majors under §2.5), not clinical advice, and I have not inflated them into Blockers.

## Majors
1. [Title; Abstract — Objectives and Selection criteria; Methods — Types of questions; Results — Description of studies; Table 1] "Primary-care presentations" does not honestly describe the assembled pool. By Table 1, 19 of the 24 primary studies are single-topic sets (Fan 2026 COPD; Miao 2026 bacterial vaginosis; Liang 2026 herpes zoster; Zheng 2026 mpox; Yu S 2026 diabetic kidney disease; Zuo 2026 traumatic brain injury; Wen 2026 diabetic foot ulcer prevention; Shi 2026 paediatric vitamin D; Gu 2026 diabetic peripheral neuropathy; Gong 2026 hand, foot and mouth disease; Zang 2026 frozen shoulder; Hao 2026 high-risk HPV follow-up; Wang C 2026 paediatric burn first aid; Lv 2026 myocardial infarction public education; An 2026 cancer survivorship; Bilgic 2026 haemorrhoids; Xiao 2026b late-life depression; Wang Y 2026 recurrent aphthous stomatitis; Zhou 2026 nutrition). Most of these are questions asked *after* a diagnosis is known — condition information, self-management, follow-up — not "presentations", which in primary care means a patient arriving with an undifferentiated problem. Several sit at or beyond the edge of what a GP fields first (myocardial infarction first aid, burn first aid, cancer survivorship, traumatic brain injury), and the only studies a community GP would recognise as a morning surgery are the mixed sets (Draelos 2026; Armbruster 2024; Ariel 2026). The Methods sentence that carries all of this weight — "Condition-level question sets that a general practitioner fields first count as primary care" — is asserted, never demonstrated study by study. What would fix it: either retitle and reframe throughout to what was actually pooled (e.g. patient-facing questions on conditions commonly managed in primary care, largely single-condition bench sets), or add a per-study justification column to Table 1 (first-contact primary care / shared care / post-diagnosis information / emergency or first aid) and report how much of the 4,868-response denominator each category contributes; the Abstract's selection criteria and the Plain language summary's "everyday health problems" must be brought into line with whichever framing is chosen.
2. [Results — Primary outcome: unsafe proportion, bench stratum; Results — Secondary outcomes: Undertriage; Table 1; extraction-settled.csv, Buis 2023 rows] Buis 2023 is an emergency-department triage study, and its "unsafe" is not the same clinical event as the primary outcome elsewhere. The settled extraction is explicit: for Buis, "'unsafe' triage means the system recommended a lower level of urgency than the physician reference, i.e. undertriage, so the same counts are entered in the undertriage field" (GPT-3.5: 15/37; GPT-4: 8/37). The manuscript never tells the reader this. The same 23 of 74 responses therefore appear in the primary unsafe pool (where Buis is singled out as the highest study, 31.1%) and again in the undertriage pool (31.1%), while the Background claims the narrow numerator rule "buys a pooled number that means the same thing in every contributing study" — for Buis it does not: it rates disposition disagreement with three ED physicians, not the content harm the other studies' tiers rate. Buis also does double duty in the era subgroup (it supplies the 40.5% early arm), so setting, era and outcome definition are tangled in the paper's two headline contrasts. What would fix it: state Buis's definition and its dual entry in both pools explicitly wherever Buis is cited; add the missing pre-emptable sensitivity — the primary pool excluding Buis 2023 (crude ex-Buis is 529/4,794, 11.0%, so this is a disclosure and interpretation problem, not one that should overturn the pooled estimate) — and soften the Background's "means the same thing in every contributing study" claim accordingly.
3. [Conclusions — Implications for practice] Two sentences step outside the data. (a) "this review supplies the base rate such an evaluation should be compared against": a GLMM estimate of 10.5% from bench sets of unlike composition, GRADE very low, with a 95% prediction interval of 3.8% to 26.2%, is not a base rate for a local service whose case mix is undifferentiated, whose patients are real, and whose model is current. A service lead who benchmarks a local audit against 10.5% has been given a number the evidence cannot carry. (b) "Rated answers to primary-care questions fall in a study's unsafe tier about one time in ten on bench sets, and more often on emergency material": the Results section is honest that the red-flag subgroup could not be fitted and that the emergency contrast "rests on one study" (Buis 2023); the Implications sentence drops both qualifiers and generalises one ED set plus small undertriage vignette sets into a property of "emergency material". What would fix it: delete or reword "base rate" to a comparison the data supports (e.g. the range of bench estimates and their prediction interval, with the very-low-certainty label attached in the same sentence), and restore the one-study / no-interaction-test qualifier to the emergency sentence, in the Implications and anywhere it recurs.
4. [Results — Description of studies; Discussion — Completeness and applicability] The applicability section omits the pool's geography, language and health-system composition entirely — the facts a primary care reader most needs. From the settled extraction, 19 of the 24 primary studies are based in China or Hong Kong (Fan, Xiao 2026a, Xiao 2026b, Miao, Liang, Wen, Shi, Gu, Zuo, Zheng, Gong, Zhou, Hao, Zang, Wang Y, Wang C, Lv and Yu S 2026 in China; An 2026 in Hong Kong), with the remainder from the United States (Buis, Draelos), Germany (Armbruster), Türkiye (Bilgic) and one multilingual study (Ariel). Description of studies reports topics and model families only. This matters clinically, not decoratively: what counts as a primary-care question, which medicines are available, and what disposition is "correct" all differ where there is no GP gatekeeping system like the one this reviewer practised in, and undertriage judgements are made against local reference dispositions. What would fix it: report country/setting (and interaction language where stated) per study in Table 1 or the Description of studies, give the aggregate composition in the Abstract-level summary of the pool, and add an applicability paragraph addressing transfer to gatekept primary care systems rather than leaving "Completeness and applicability" to databases and question provenance alone.
5. [Objectives; Methods — Outcome measures; Results — Secondary outcomes: Undertriage] The disposition framing is one-sided in a way that would mislead a clinician about what good triage behaviour is. Undertriage is pooled and discussed, but overtriage — the "too cautious" category that Buis's own 3-level scale records, and the natural balancing harm (every uncertain patient sent to emergency care is safe by this outcome and unusable in practice) — is never reported, defined as an outcome, or mentioned anywhere in the manuscript. Two further clinical caveats are missing: a lower disposition than the reference is labelled undertriage on the assumption the reference disposition is correct, with no discussion that bench ratings cannot capture safety-netting advice in the same answer; and the largest undertriage contributor's red-flag stratum is absent — Harada 2026 contributes 0/480 mild and 0/1,440 non-red-flag slices "with the red-flag numerator not printed" — yet the section concludes that "undertriage concentrates in emergency and red-flag material", a concentration partly manufactured by the missing red-flag numerator in the one large study. The decision not to summarise undertriage by its pooled mean (prediction interval 0.1% to 96.1%) is correct and I endorse it. What would fix it: add overtriage/too-cautious reporting wherever the source studies print it, or state explicitly that it was not extractable and why that limits any disposition conclusion; qualify the "concentrates in emergency and red-flag material" conclusion with the Harada gap in the same paragraph.
6. [Results — Primary outcome: human-user stratum] The Si 2026 comparison is presented in a form a clinical reader will take as real-world comparative performance: "inappropriate medication recommendations appeared in 336 of 496 LLM encounters (67.7%…) against 58 of 248 encounters with primary care physicians (23.4%)". The arithmetic checks (see Numbers checked), and the following sentence says these outcomes "answer a different question", but nothing tells the reader what the extraction sheet shows: this is a standardised-patient field experiment in 62 primary health care institutions in China, on unstable angina and asthma presentations, in a 2026 preprint, and a 23.4% "inappropriate medication" rate for practising physicians itself signals that the definition and setting are highly specific rather than transportable. Placed in the primary-outcome Results with no such caveat, the near three-fold contrast is the single most quotable — and most misleading — clinical comparison in the paper. What would fix it: in the same paragraph, name the design, setting, presentations, preprint status and the outcome's definition-dependence, and state explicitly that it is not an estimate of how LLM advice compares with primary care physicians in routine practice.

## Minors
1. [Abstract — Selection criteria; Methods — Types of questions; Table 1] The mental-health boundary is unexplained. Crisis and self-harm sets are "excluded from the pools", yet the primary pool includes Xiao 2026a (psychological support for depression and anxiety) and Xiao 2026b (late-life depression, 29/270 unsafe) — from a practice standpoint late-life depression is exactly where crisis content is hardest to separate. State the operational line that was applied (what in a depression set would have made it a "crisis" set) and whether either Xiao set contained self-harm items.
2. [Methods — Types of questions] The coordinator scope ruling that excluded "procedure-pathway sets, subspecialty emergency-protocol sets and specialist-management sets" is not reconciled with what stayed in: an emergency-department triage set (Buis 2023), myocardial infarction public education (Lv 2026) and paediatric burn first aid (Wang C 2026). A short worked example of the ruling applied to these three borderline cases would let a reader judge the boundary instead of trusting it.
3. [Background, paragraph 1] "Primary care is where most of these questions belong" is asserted without a source. In community practice many of these questions go first to a pharmacist, an urgent-care or emergency service, a specialist the patient is already under, or nowhere clinical at all. Either cite support or soften to the claim the review needs (that primary care is a natural frame), which the following sentences already make.
4. [Plain language summary] "everyday health problems" understates the pool in the opposite direction from Major 1: myocardial infarction, mpox, traumatic brain injury and paediatric burns are in these question sets. A lay reader deserves the same corrected framing as the abstract, in plain words.
5. [Results — Narrative set] "human-user studies report that people often follow chatbot advice, including advice the study team judged wrong, while rating their own confidence highly" gives no study, count or denominator for "often". In a paper whose whole discipline is tying claims to counts, name the studies and what they actually measured, or downgrade "often" to what the cited studies show.
6. [Methods — Types of studies / Outcome measures; §2.10 audience] "Bench" is used in the Abstract and Summary of findings before it is defined (Methods, Types of studies), and "disposition" is used from the Objectives onward without a plain gloss. A reader one specialty over — the stated audience test — should not have to infer either. One clause each at first use fixes both.

## Nits
1. [Plain language summary] "about 3 in 10" for Buis 2023's 31.1% is fair rounding, but labelling it only "an emergency triage set" in the lay summary, while the condition sets around it are described as everyday problems, buries the setting contrast that Major 2 asks to have surfaced; name it as emergency-department triage.
2. [Table 1] The topic labels would serve a clinical reader better in a consistent form (condition as the patient would name it, then the study's framing); e.g. "Psychological support in chronic illness" (Xiao 2026a) is a study aim, not a topic, in a column where every other entry is a condition.
3. [Discussion — Summary of main results] "Variation between question sets is the larger story" is the most clinically honest sentence in the Discussion; consider promoting its content (prediction interval, the 0% sets, the emergency set) into the Authors' conclusions, which currently lead with the single pooled figure.

## Closing paragraph
If this paper is published as it stands, the worst true thing a critical reader could say about it is that it measured chatbot answers to investigator-written, single-disease question banks — most of them from one country's research groups, several of them about care that does not start in a GP's room, and one of them an emergency-department triage exercise whose "unsafe" answers are the same events it counts again as undertriage — and then published the result under the name of primary-care presentations, with an implications section that offers the pooled figure to service leads as "the base rate" for their own decisions. Every element of that sentence is documented in the paper's own tables and extraction sheet, which is precisely why the paper does not need to fear the correction: the underlying work is unusually transparent about its bench-only evidence, its empty human-user stratum and its very low certainty, and a version that narrows its title and implications to the pool it actually assembled would be a genuinely useful warning to primary care. Published unrevised, it hands its critics the framing, and risks the 10.5% figure being quoted in consulting rooms and commissioning meetings as if it described the next patient who walks in with an undifferentiated problem and a chatbot answer in their hand.
