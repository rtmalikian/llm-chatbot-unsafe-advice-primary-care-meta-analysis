# Reviewer report (Gate 2)

Paper: Unsafe medical advice from patient-facing large language model chatbots in primary-care presentations: a systematic review and meta-analysis (Review 4)
Reviewer (persona and bucket): Reviewer 2, Bucket C — Meta-analysis statistician, 14 years in trials and meta-analysis consultancy. Sceptical of pooled proportions. Calibrated bias: under-values clinical importance, over-values statistical purity; I do not rule on clinical importance anywhere in this report.
Date: 2026-11-13

## Summary (5 to 8 sentences, in your frame)

The counting underneath this paper is unusually well kept: I re-summed every pool from `stage3-extraction/extraction-settled.csv` and from `stage4-analysis/analysis-dataset.csv` and every headline crude total, unit count and study count reproduces exactly. The modelling layered on top of that counting is where the paper is exposed. The primary GLMM estimate (10.5%) and the Hartung–Knapp sensitivity (12.5%) differ by more than the entire range of the four pre-specified sensitivities, yet only the GLMM figure is headlined and the Hartung–Knapp values that exist for the sensitivities and for both secondary pools are nowhere in the manuscript. Worse, the confidence intervals reported for the two heterogeneous secondary pools are mathematically incompatible with the paper's own tau² values and unit counts, which means the standard-error machinery in the custom Python code has failed exactly where heterogeneity is largest. All pools also treat model arms as independent units although up to six arms share one study's question set and raters, so every interval and the one interaction p-value are anti-conservative. The era interaction (p = 0.001) rests on 3 arms from 2 studies and silently drops 4 further arms whose era was not reported. None of this is fatal to the paper's counting, but the inferential layer needs to be refitted in validated software, with clustering handled, before any number is submitted.

## Overall recommendation
Major revision. Main reason: one Blocker — the problematic-tier confidence interval carried in the abstract, Summary of findings and Results (18.7%, 95% CI 17.2 to 20.2) cannot be a valid random-effects interval given the paper's own tau² = 1.05 and k = 38 units (see Blocker 1), and the same failure affects the undertriage interval. Until the pools are refitted with validated code and the estimator, clustering and era questions below are answered in the text, the headline inferential claims should not ship.

## Blockers
1. [Abstract, Main results; Summary of findings table; Results, "Problematic tier"; stage4-analysis/analysis-results.json] The GLMM confidence intervals for the heterogeneous pools are invalid on the paper's own numbers. For a random-effects mean on the logit scale, Var(mu) ≥ tau²/k, so SE(mu) ≥ sqrt(tau²/k). For S1 (problematic): tau² = 1.0517, k = 38, so SE ≥ 0.166; the reported CI 17.23–20.23% around 18.69% implies SE = 0.050 — one third of the minimum possible value. For S2 (undertriage): tau² = 4.963, k = 12, so SE ≥ 0.643; the reported CI 11.87–17.75% around 14.57% implies SE = 0.120 — under one fifth of the minimum. These intervals cannot have come from a correctly fitted random-effects model with the stated tau²; the finite-difference Hessian standard errors in `analysis.py` (eps = 1e-4, evaluated at a Nelder–Mead optimum of the quadrature objective, inverted via pseudo-inverse) have failed in the high-tau² regime. The S1 interval is a headline number (abstract, Summary of findings, Results, Discussion "about twice the unsafe rate"), so under Protocol Rule 6 and §2.4 it blocks submission. The primary pool's interval passes the same check (implied SE 0.074 vs bound 0.057), but it was produced by the same unvalidated machinery and cannot be trusted by association. What would fix it: refit all pools in validated software (e.g. metafor `rma.glmm`, for which `analysis.R` already contains the intended call, or an equivalent checked implementation), report the refitted point estimates, CIs, tau² and prediction intervals everywhere the current values appear, and state in Methods which implementation is the analysis of record. If the refit moves any headline value, the abstract and conclusions move with it.

## Majors
1. [Abstract; Results, primary outcome; Table 2; Discussion] The GLMM–Hartung–Knapp gap is larger than every sensitivity the paper offers as reassurance, and the manuscript headlines only one side of it. GLMM 10.5% (9.2 to 12.0) vs Hartung–Knapp 12.5% (11.3 to 13.9): a 2.0 percentage-point gap whose intervals overlap only between 11.3 and 12.0, against a four-sensitivity range of 10.0–10.6% that the abstract and Discussion ("barely moves") present as stability. Estimator choice is the single largest source of uncertainty in the primary result and it is currently resolved by fiat. Compounding this, the Hartung–Knapp results that were actually computed for the four P1 sensitivities (12.36, 12.69, 12.18, 12.54 in analysis-results.json) and for S1 (19.33%, 13.76 to 26.47) and S2 (11.54%, 3.21 to 33.86) appear nowhere in the manuscript, although Methods (Synthesis) promises the Hartung–Knapp fit "is reported as a sensitivity wherever a pool holds at least four units" — S1 (38 units) and S2 (12 units) both qualify. I verified by text search: none of 19.3, 13.76, 26.47, 11.54, 3.21 or 33.86 appears in the manuscript. What would fix it: either justify the GLMM as the sole headline estimator on stated grounds and report the Hartung–Knapp result alongside it in the abstract and Table 2 (including for the sensitivities and secondary pools, as Methods promises), or present both estimators as co-primary. The "stable across sensitivities" claim must be reworded to acknowledge that it holds within one estimator only.
2. [Methods, Synthesis; all pools; Table 2] No allowance for within-study clustering: 95 arms from 23 studies are fitted as 95 independent units. Arms within a study share the question set, the raters, the rating scale and the setting, so they are not independent data points; the effective information is closer to 23 studies than to 95 arms. Every CI, the prediction interval (computed with a t critical value on k − 2 = 93 df, where k counts arms), and the era interaction test inherit this anti-conservatism. As an illustrative check I re-pooled the primary outcome at study level (arms aggregated within study, DerSimonian–Laird logit with Hartung–Knapp, 23 units): 11.7% (9.3 to 14.5) — a materially wider interval than either headline interval. What would fix it: fit a model with a study-level random effect (three-level / nested arm-within-study GLMM) or, at minimum, add a pre-specified-style study-level aggregated sensitivity for the primary pool and report it in Table 2, and recompute prediction intervals with degrees of freedom based on studies, not arms.
3. [Abstract; Results, Subgroup analyses; Table 2] The era interaction is over-claimed for the data underneath it. The early level is 3 arms from 2 studies (Armbruster 2024 GPT-4 at 16/100; Buis 2023 GPT-3.5 at 15/37 and GPT-4 at 8/37 — I verified these in the settled sheet). With two clusters in one level, era and study are fully confounded — the text concedes this qualitatively — yet the abstract still carries "interaction p = 0.001", and that p-value comes from a Wald z-test on Hessian standard errors in the same arm-level model criticised in Blocker 1 and Major 2, with no small-sample adjustment. A p-value quoted to three decimal places on an effective two-study contrast is not a finding. Two further undisclosed facts: (i) 3 + 88 = 91, not 95 — 4 arms (Wang 2026, three arms, 8/90; An 2026 HK Chat arm, 7/50; crude 15/140, 10.7%) have era "not_reported" in analysis-dataset.csv and are silently excluded from both the subgroup and the "current-era" sensitivity, which is therefore not "current-era arms only" but "era-classified late arms only"; (ii) the era-late sensitivity (10.2%, 88 arms) is numerically the same fit as the era-late subgroup level, so Table 2 presents one analysis twice as if it were two corroborations. What would fix it: demote the era contrast to a descriptive, hypothesis-generating paragraph with no p-value in the abstract (or refit with a cluster-appropriate test and report the resulting, certainly much larger, p-value), disclose the 4 unclassified arms and where they were excluded, and label the duplication between the subgroup level and the sensitivity.
4. [Methods, Data extraction; Results, primary and problematic; Table 2] Derived counts dominate the pools and their scale is never stated. Re-summing from the settled sheet and analysis dataset: in the primary pool, 34 of 95 arms (35.8%) are flagged DERIVED, contributing 246 of 552 unsafe events (44.6%) and 2,247 of 4,868 responses (46.2%). In the problematic pool the dependence is near-total: 27 of 38 units are derived, contributing 2,023 of 2,147 events (94.2%) and 7,156 of 8,190 responses (87.4%) — including all five Khan 2026 arms (1,191 events; Khan alone supplies 5,000 of 8,190 responses, 61% of the denominator) and both Si 2026 arms. A reader is told a derived-count sensitivity exists for the primary pool but is never told that excluding derived arms discards over a third of the arms and nearly half the events, nor that the no-derived refit is less precise in ways the point estimate hides: tau² rises from 0.30 to 0.52 and the prediction interval widens from 3.8–26.2% to 2.5–32.4% (results-summary.csv), neither reported. No derived-count sensitivity exists for S1 at all, where it matters most. What would fix it: state the derived share (arms, events, responses) for each pool in Results; report tau² and the prediction interval for the no-derived primary sensitivity alongside its point estimate; add and report a no-derived sensitivity for the problematic pool, or explain why the pool cannot survive one.
5. [Methods, Synthesis and Data collection; stage4-analysis/analysis.py and analysis-output.md] The estimator as described is not the estimator as run, and the one internal check that exposes this is not reported. The manuscript (twice) and the analysis plan specify "adaptive Gauss–Hermite quadrature (15 nodes)". The code in `analysis.py` (`glmm_fit`) places fixed Gauss–Hermite nodes around the fixed effect with scale tau — there is no per-unit posterior mode or scale adaptation; the code comment even promises a Laplace/grid adaptation the code does not perform. Non-adaptive quadrature is at its least reliable precisely for the sparse and zero-event arms this pool contains. The filed independent refit (analysis-output.md, hand-check 1) returned tau² = 0.20 by grid integration against 0.304 by the main script — a one-third relative discrepancy in the parameter that drives the prediction interval (at tau² = 0.20 the primary prediction interval would be roughly 4.6 to 22.4% rather than 3.8 to 26.2%) — while the manuscript reports only the reassuring agreement of the point estimates (10.55% vs 10.52%) and calls the likelihood "flat in tau²" only in the internal output, not in the paper. What would fix it: correct the Methods description to what was actually computed, or (preferably, with Blocker 1) refit in validated software; report the tau² discrepancy between computation routes in the manuscript and carry the resulting prediction-interval uncertainty honestly.
6. [Abstract, Selection criteria; Results, Problematic tier; Summary of findings] The problematic pool mixes strata and unit types that the paper elsewhere says were kept apart, without disclosure. The abstract states bench and human-user studies "were kept apart at every stage", but analysis-dataset.csv shows the S1 pool contains the two human-user arms of Si 2026 (ChatGPT-4o 182/248, 73.4%; DeepSeek-R1 154/248, 62.1% — both derived, and the two highest-rate units in the pool) pooled together with 36 bench units. The pool also mixes arm-level units with study-slice units from citation chasing, on tier definitions the authors themselves describe as unlike ("an intermediate accuracy grade in one, a combined error tier in another"), with I² = 96.6% and a prediction interval of 2.8 to 64.8%. The undertriage outcome in the same position was (rightly) demoted to study-by-study reporting because "a pooled mean over these parts describes none of them"; the problematic pool meets the same description and is instead headlined with a two-decimal-place-style precision its (invalid, see Blocker 1) interval only appears to offer. What would fix it: disclose the Si 2026 human-user units inside S1 (or remove them to their own stratum, per the paper's stated rule), state the unit definition for every S1 unit, and either demote the problematic pool to the same descriptive treatment as undertriage or justify explicitly why pooling is defensible here and not there.
7. [Results, primary outcome and Undertriage; Table 1] The same events are counted in two pools without disclosure. In Buis 2023 the study's "unsafe" triage tier is defined by undertriage, and the settled extraction records identical counts for both outcomes (GPT-3.5: unsafe 15/37 and undertriage 15/37; GPT-4: 8/37 and 8/37). The same 23 events therefore enter the primary pool as unsafe responses and the undertriage pool as undertriage responses — 23 of the undertriage pool's 120 events (19%) are primary-pool events under a second label — and the manuscript quotes the identical 31.1% (23/74) in both the primary results and the undertriage results, and again in the red-flag subgroup paragraph, without ever telling the reader they are the same responses counted under a definition that makes the two outcomes coincide for this study. What would fix it: a sentence in each of the primary and undertriage results stating that for Buis 2023 the unsafe tier is an undertriage definition and the counts are the same events, plus a footnote in Table 1; and a short justification for admitting a triage-disposition definition to a primary pool whose stated rule is an explicit unsafe/harmful/dangerous tier.
8. [Results, primary outcome; stage4-analysis/analysis-output.md] The explanation of the GLMM–Hartung–Knapp gap misdescribes the sensitivity's mechanics and the zero-event census is incomplete. The manuscript and analysis output attribute the gap to the zero-event arms "(Wen 2026, 0 of 100 across five arms; Bilgic 2026, 0 of 75), which the GLMM handles with no continuity correction". In fact there are nine zero-event arms in the primary pool — the dataset also contains Xiao 2026a's ChatGPT arm at 0/20 — and `hk_sensitivity` in analysis.py applies the 0.5 correction to every arm, not only to zero-event arms (yi = log((y + 0.5)/(n − y + 0.5)) for all 95 arms), which shifts small arms generally, not just the zero ones. The two fits also estimate quite different heterogeneity (DerSimonian–Laird tau² = 0.14 on the corrected logits, by my recomputation, vs GLMM tau² = 0.30), which is part of the weighting story and is reported nowhere. What would fix it: correct the zero-event census (9 arms, 195 responses), describe the correction as applying to all arms in the sensitivity as coded, and report the sensitivity's tau² alongside the GLMM's so the weighting explanation can be judged.

## Minors
1. [Results, all pools] I² is presented as if it were a product of the GLMM; in `analysis.py` it is computed separately (`i2_logit`) as a fixed-effect Q statistic on 0.5-corrected logits. The two heterogeneity summaries therefore come from different models, which is nowhere stated. For single proportions I² is also strongly driven by arm sizes (near-inevitable inflation where arms have n = 1,000, as in Khan 2026), and the primary pool's I² of 42.0% sits awkwardly beside a prediction interval spanning 3.8 to 26.2% — a seven-fold ratio — without comment. Label the I² method, and let tau² and the prediction interval carry the heterogeneity message.
2. [Table 1] The arms column sums to 100, not 95: it includes Ariel 2026's 4 arms (which contribute no analysed arm) and Draelos 2026's figure-only Gemini arm (the Draelos row reads "4" arms where 3 were analysed, 70/666). The reconciliation is available in the text but a reader summing the table will not find it. Add a table footnote marking the five unanalysed arms.
3. [Results, Problematic tier] The main-search-only sensitivity is reported as "18.4% (17.2 to 19.8) on 23 units" without its study count: results-summary.csv shows those 23 units come from only 8 studies, while the other 15 units come from 9 chasing studies. The chasing contribution to this pool is thus far larger than to the primary pool (zero chasing studies) and should be stated wherever the pool's composition is summarised.
4. [Results, Undertriage] "Units" in the undertriage pool are not a consistent entity: Harada 2026's two units are two presentation slices of a single model arm (gpt-4o-mini, 0/480 and 0/1,440), Brown 2025 contributes two units of n = 10 each, and the remaining units are model arms. State the per-unit definition in the figure or table legend, since the pool's behaviour (pooled mean 14.6% against a crude 4.5%) turns on it.
5. [Table 2] The era interaction row reports the coefficient (−1.03) and p-value with no standard error or confidence interval for the interaction itself. Report the interval, or (per Major 3) remove the test from the table.
6. [Abstract vs Results] The crude primary proportion (11.3%) — the one number a reader can verify from 552/4,868 without any model — appears in the Results but not in the abstract, where it would usefully bracket the GLMM (10.5%) and Hartung–Knapp (12.5%) values. Add it.

## Nits
1. [Results, primary outcome] The independent refit is reported as "returned 10.6%, against 10.5% from the main script"; the filed values are 10.55% and 10.52%, an agreement of 0.03 percentage points that one-decimal rounding inflates to an apparent 0.1. Quote two decimals for this comparison.
2. [Summary of findings] The undertriage row gives the crude rate (4.5%) in a comment while the text reports a GLMM pooled mean of 14.6% that the authors disown; a reader skimming the table alone will take away neither number cleanly. Consider "not pooled — see text" in the pooled-proportion column.
3. [Table 2] Sensitivity rows would be easier to audit with events/totals (e.g. 306/2,621 for the no-derived fit) beside the arm counts; all are available in analysis-results.json.

## Closing paragraph
If this paper is published as it stands, what is the worst true thing a critical reader could say about it? That its most-quoted precision is manufactured: the problematic-tier estimate is printed with a confidence interval (17.2 to 20.2%) that is arithmetically impossible for the random-effects model the paper itself reports, the primary interval treats 95 model arms as if they were 95 independent studies when there are only 23, the one p-value in the abstract (0.001) comes from a subgroup whose early level contains two studies, and the headline 10.5% was chosen over the equally pre-specified 12.5% without a stated reason — so the paper's counting, which is meticulous and fully reproducible, is dressed in inferential clothing that its own analysis files do not support.
