# Re-review, Gate 5, loop 1

Paper: Unsafe or potentially unsafe advice from patient-facing large language model chatbots in bench evaluations of primary-care question sets: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Reviewer: Reviewer 4 — Patient/community reviewer (Bucket E). I am a layperson. I have used general chatbots for health questions for myself and for family members. I am re-checking this paper from the patient's seat only.
Date: 13 November 2026
What I re-read: my own first report, the rows about my comments in the response matrix, and the revised manuscript — above all the title, the abstract, the plain language summary and the conclusions, because those are the parts a patient will actually read.

## Short summary

The front of this paper is much safer for a patient reader than it was. The warning now comes before the number, not after it. The plain language summary says, in its first lines, that this is about test questions, that it is not the chance an answer I received was unsafe, and that it is not a safety rating for any chatbot I can open today. It says the certainty is very low and explains what that means. It says crisis questions were left out. It says real-user studies do exist and gives their main finding. It tells a worried reader where to take a worry.

One of my Major points is only partly fixed, and it is a simple one to finish. The paper now says plainly, in the methods and in the declarations, that no patient or member of the public was involved at any stage. But it does not say that in the discussion as a limitation, even though the methods claim that it does. I checked the discussion myself: the sentence is not there.

Counts, so the editor can use them at a glance. I filed 14 rows in all: one line recording that I found no Blocker, 6 Majors, 4 Minors and 3 Nits. Of those 14 rows: **Resolved 11, Partly resolved 2, Unresolved 1.** Leaving the no-Blocker line aside, that is 10 Resolved, 2 Partly resolved and 1 Unresolved out of my 13 graded comments. I found **no Blocker, and no new Major** caused by the revision — see the last section.

## Blockers

**R4-1 — No Blocker in my area. Status: Resolved (checked again, still none).**
I found no Blocker the first time, for three reasons: the big gap was stated openly, the paper gives no advice to any one patient about their own care, and it does not clear or rank any chatbot a reader can open today. All three still hold in the revised text. The one new sentence for worried readers — check an answer you have followed with your own doctor, pharmacist or local urgent care service — is general information, not advice to one person, and it is the right kind of sentence to have added.

## Majors

**Major 1 — The headline "1 in 10 unsafe" will be read as a personal risk, and the parts that stop that reading come too late. Status: Resolved.**
This is fixed in the places a patient meets the number. The title now says "in bench evaluations of primary-care question sets", so the title on its own no longer promises a measure of real patient use. The plain language summary now opens with the limit, before any number appears: "This review is about test questions, not about answers real patients received", "It is not the chance that an answer you received was unsafe", and "it is not a safety rating for any chatbot product you can open today". The abstract's conclusions start the same way — "In bench evaluations, about one response in ten..." — and the practice section puts the words "on bench sets" next to "about one time in ten". A reader would now have to skip the warning to take the number home as a personal risk. The summary also gives the figure as "1 in 10 to 1 in 8, depending on the method used to combine the studies", which is more honest than one tidy number.

**Major 2 — The plain language summary gives "about 1 in 10" without the authors' own warning that the certainty is very low, and without the spread. Status: Resolved.**
The plain language summary now says, next to the figure, in plain words: "The authors rate their certainty in the 1 in 10 figure as very low, which means the true figure could be quite different." It also gives the spread I asked for: some sets produced no unsafe answers at all, one emergency department triage set produced about 3 in 10, and across new question sets the share could run from about 5 in 100 to about 23 in 100. Those figures match the revised prediction interval of 4.5 to 22.7% printed in the abstract. A lay reader no longer gets a tidy number with no sense of how weakly the authors themselves hold it.

**Major 3 — The "no real patients" point is stated, but the wording can mislead the other way, and what real-person studies did find is missing where patients will look. Status: Resolved.**
The two statements I asked to be kept apart are now apart, in the places I named. The plain language summary says no study in the main count followed real patients and graded the answers those patients actually got, one by one — and in a later paragraph it says, in its own heading sentence, "Studies of real users do exist. They looked at what people decided and did, rather than grading each answer." It then gives the main finding in plain words: in the study that used trained pretend patients in China, inappropriate medication recommendations appeared in about two thirds of chatbot visits, against about one quarter of visits with primary care doctors, on that study's own definitions. The abstract does the same job in its own order: its main results give that finding with its counts (336 of 496 chatbot encounters, 67.7%, against 58 of 248, 23.4%), and its authors' conclusions say that studies with real users exist and report what people decided and did, but do not report rated-answer safety. The Results section on the human-user stratum carries the same finding with its setting and its warning that it is not a measure of ordinary practice. A patient is no longer told, in effect, that nobody has ever studied real people.

**Major 4 — The plain language summary never says the scariest questions are outside the main count, and undertriage is not told to the lay reader. Status: Resolved.**
The plain language summary now has a paragraph that starts "Two things the main count does not cover matter." First: "questions about a mental-health crisis or self-harm were kept out of the main count, so the 1 in 10 figure says nothing about questions like those." That is exactly the sentence the parent reading this paper at 2 a.m. needed. Second, it explains being told to stay home when you should seek urgent care, names it as undertriage, says it was counted apart, and says that in small emergency studies chatbots advised a lower level of care than they should have in a large share of cases. The abstract's selection criteria and the summary of findings table back both points up for a reader who goes further.

**Major 5 — I cannot tell whether people like my family are in this evidence at all. Status: Resolved.**
The paper now tells me, and it does not dress the answer up. The description of studies says how the question sets were made, that the language of the questions is recorded unevenly — one study bilingual, one using Japanese vignettes, one in six languages, and the rest not stating the language beyond the interface used — and that no pool study sampled older users as a group, people with low health literacy, or people with low digital confidence. The discussion's "Completeness and applicability" section says the same thing in the place I asked for it: the evidence "does not describe older users, people asking in a second language, or people with low health literacy or low digital confidence", the pooled result is not claimed to carry to them, and most of the primary studies (19 of 24) come from China or Hong Kong, which the abstract now states too. An honest paragraph about the gap was all I asked for. It is there.

**Major 6 — The counted outcome is harm as doctors see it, and no patient was in the room when "unsafe" was defined. Status: Partly resolved.**
What is fixed is real, and it is plainly worded. The methods now have a headed section that begins "There was none. No patient or member of the public helped choose the review question, define what counts as harm, or read the plain language summary." The declarations say the same thing in one line: "None, at any stage: not in the question, the harm definition, the analysis or the plain language summary." The methods also carry the words I asked for about the counted harm: it is "clinician-graded harm on paper, not harm as patients experienced it".
What remains, in plain words, is this. That methods section ends by saying "This is named as a limitation in the Discussion" — and it is not. I read the discussion through, section by section (summary of main results, completeness and applicability, quality of the evidence, agreements with other reviews, biases in the review process, and the author's position). None of them says that no patient was involved, or that the harm counted is harm as doctors graded it rather than harm as patients lived it. So the one place the paper sends a reader to for this limitation does not contain it. A patient who reads only the plain language summary, the abstract or the conclusions will also never learn that no patient helped define "unsafe" or read the summary written for patients. The fix left to do is small and exact: put one plain sentence in the discussion itself, as a named limitation, saying there was no patient or public involvement and that the harm counted is clinician-graded harm on paper — because at the moment the paper only says that such a sentence is there, and saying so is not the same as it being there.

## Minors

**Minor 1 — The counts do not line up for a lay reader: 23 studies in the summary, 24 in the abstract. Status: Resolved.**
The plain language summary now explains it in one clause, as I suggested: "A 24th study could not be counted because it did not publish its numbers answer by answer." The 23 and the 24 now sit side by side with the reason between them.

**Minor 2 — Words a lay reader has to guess at in the parts meant for them, and the graders-could-see point missing. Status: Resolved.**
The abstract now explains "bench" where it first uses it — studies in which investigators put test questions to a chatbot and clinician raters grade the answers afterwards — and explains "response-level" in ordinary words in the main results, as ratings of each answer a real user received. The plain language summary avoids the jargon words I named and puts the spread in "about 5 in 100 to about 23 in 100" instead of naming a prediction interval. It also adds the fairness point, in words anyone grasps at once: "In some studies the graders could see which chatbot wrote each answer, which can sway a grade."

**Minor 3 — One sentence about real users reads as blaming the user. Status: Resolved.**
The sentence has been reworded in both places it appears, in the plain language summary and in the narrative results, so the burden sits on the product: "answers are written in a confident style, and that people tended to follow them, including answers the study team judged wrong." That is the order of cause a patient reader needs — the answer sounds sure of itself first, and the following comes second.

**Minor 4 — Nothing for the reader who is now worried about an answer they already acted on. Status: Resolved.**
The plain language summary now ends its advice with a general signpost, and it is general information rather than advice to any one person: "If you are worried about an answer you have already followed, check it with your own doctor, pharmacist or local urgent care service rather than judging it from this paper." That is the sentence I asked for, in the place a worried reader will find it.

## Nits

**Nit 1 — "Questions they once brought to primary care" quietly assumes the reader had a doctor they could ask. Status: Partly resolved.**
What is fixed: the plain language summary no longer makes that assumption — it speaks of "health questions of the kind people take to a family doctor" — and the background section now frames chatbot use by cost and hour ("at no cost and at any hour") and says primary care is the frame for questions a family doctor fields first "when a patient can reach one". What remains, in plain words, is one unchanged sentence: the abstract's background still says patients now ask chatbots "the questions they once brought to primary care". For a reader who never had a doctor they could bring a question to, that line still reads as if the choice was theirs. Changing those few words in the abstract is all that is left of this point.

**Nit 2 — "Primary-care presentations" is professional language; the lay wording should be the one used in press or social wording. Status: Unresolved.**
I said in my first report that the technical title itself could stay if the journal requires that form, and I am not changing that view — the title is not the problem, and it has in any case been changed for other reasons. What I asked for was that the plain version — the kind of problems you would take to your family doctor — be the wording used in any press or social wording. Nothing in the revised manuscript does that, and nothing in it records that it will be done. The only answer on this point is a note in the response matrix saying it has been recorded. Under this gate's own rule, an answer in words only, with no change a reader can check, counts as not answered. It stays a Nit: it blocks nothing, and it can be closed the day a plain press wording is actually written down with the paper's files.

**Nit 3 — The word "laypeople" is used for people like me. Status: Resolved.**
The manuscript's own text now says "members of the public" where it describes human-user studies, and "member of the public" in the involvement statement. The only place "layperson" still appears is inside the printed title of one cited paper in the reference list, which is that paper's title and not this paper's word for its readers. That is as it should be.

## New Majors raised at re-review

**None.** I state this plainly, as the gate requires: the revision introduced no new Major problem for a patient reader. The new wording I found fault with — the methods line claiming a limitation is named in the discussion when it is not — is not a new point. It is the unfinished half of my original Major 6, and I have counted it there, not twice. The wider headline the revision now gives (1 in 10 to 1 in 8, with several combined figures in the abstract) is harder reading than one number, but the plain language summary explains it in ordinary words, and a harder-but-honester front page is not a fault from the patient's seat.

## Closing word

If this paper were published tomorrow, the worst true thing a critical reader could still say from a patient's point of view is much narrower than it was: a patient can now see, before the number, what the number is and is not — but no patient was in the room when "unsafe" was defined, and the paper still does not admit that in the one section, its discussion, where it claims to admit it. Fix that one sentence, and change the few words left in Nit 1, and from this seat the paper's front, its summary and its conclusions no longer mislead the reader they are most likely to reach.
