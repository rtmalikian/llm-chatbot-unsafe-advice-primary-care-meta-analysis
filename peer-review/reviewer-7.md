# Reviewer report (Gate 2)

Paper: Unsafe medical advice from patient-facing large language model chatbots in primary-care presentations: a systematic review and meta-analysis (Review 4)
Reviewer (persona and bucket): Reviewer 7 — Bucket I, THE SCEPTIC. Senior clinical AI researcher, 20 years. Stance, as assigned: the headline claim is probably overstated, and my job is the strongest honest case against it built from the paper's own evidence only. Every objection below cites a section, table, or file in the review package.
Date: 2026-11-13

## Summary (5 to 8 sentences, in your frame)

I have spent twenty years watching safety percentages for clinical AI get quoted long after the conditions that produced them are forgotten, and this paper hands the quoters a very quotable number: 10.5%. My problem is not the arithmetic — I re-summed the pool and the arithmetic is right — it is what the number is a percentage *of*. On the review's own extraction sheet, the "explicit unsafe, harmful or dangerous tier" that defines the primary numerator turns out, in most contributing studies, to be a *potentially* unsafe tier, a triage-discordance label, or a "major error" grade that includes errors defined as only potentially consequential; the pooled figure therefore does not measure what the title, abstract and conclusions say it measures. The headline also consistently selects the most favourable of the paper's own estimates: the pre-specified GLMM gives 10.5%, but the Hartung–Knapp fit gives 12.5% for the main pool and 12.2–12.7% in every sensitivity, the crude rate is 11.3%, and the abstract's reassurance that the estimate "holds at 10.0 to 10.6% across four sensitivities" is true only inside the one estimator that produces the lowest number. Nearly half the pooled responses (2,247 of 4,868) rest on derived rather than printed counts, and the tight confidence interval does not survive their removal even though the point estimate does. The one subgroup "finding" (era, p = 0.001) rests on 3 arms from 2 studies, both at high risk of bias, one of them the emergency-triage outlier and the other a study whose responses were edited and given a physician-role prompt — and the contrast only exists because the protocol's model-generation subgroup was silently replaced by a release-date cut-off. Meanwhile 121 of the 166 included studies — the set containing the only evidence about what real users do with this advice — are dismissed in a single paragraph while the 23-study bench pool takes the title, the abstract and the conclusions. The dataset underneath all this is unusually well documented, and most of what I ask for is already in the authors' own files; but as written, the paper's claims run ahead of its evidence in exactly the direction a sceptic would predict.

## Overall recommendation
Major revision. Main reason: the headline construct is mislabelled (Blocker 1) — the pool counts predominantly *potentially* unsafe tiers while the title, abstract and conclusions report them as "unsafe" — and the reporting systematically favours the lowest available estimate, promotes a confounded 3-arm subgroup contrast, and headlines a 23-study bench pool over the 121-study narrative set that contains the human-behaviour evidence. None of this requires new data collection; all of it requires the claims to be cut back to what the filed evidence supports, plus the re-analyses and tables specified below.

## Blockers

1. **[Abstract; Background, para 3; extraction-settled.csv (`unsafe_tier_label`, `rating_scale`); GRADE assessment, Outcome 1] The pooled numerator is not "unsafe" — in most studies it is "potentially unsafe," and the paper's central poolability claim is contradicted by its own extraction sheet.** The Background claims the eligibility line "buys a pooled number that means the same thing in every contributing study, within the limits of each study's own tier definition." The settled extraction sheet shows it does not. The tier labels actually pooled include: "potentially unsafe" (Liang 2026; Gong 2026; Zang 2026; Yu S 2026), "unsafe or potentially unsafe" (Miao 2026; Gu 2026), "potentially unsafe or potentially harmful" (Zuo 2026; Zhou 2026), "potentially unsafe or misleading" (Wang Y 2026 — *misleading* is pooled as unsafe), "potential harm" (Hao 2026), "potentially harmful advice" (Armbruster 2024), "major clinical or safety error" (Xiao 2026a/b), "major safety error (grades 2 and 3)" where grade 2 is defined as a "potentially consequential error" (Wen 2026), "critical error" on a global quality scale (Bilgic 2026), and "unsafe (triage)" in Buis 2023, which is not a harm rating at all but disagreement with a physician reference disposition — the identical 23/74 from Buis 2023 is also pooled as *undertriage* in pool S2, so the same events do duty as two different outcomes in two pools without the manuscript disclosing the overlap. Zheng 2026's own post-hoc severity reassessment, recorded on the extraction sheet, grades some of its "unsafe" responses as "0 = no meaningful potential harm." A tier that its own study re-grades as carrying no meaningful potential for harm is in the numerator of a pooled proportion the conclusions report as "about one response in ten … was rated unsafe." The authors know the definitions differ — the GRADE worksheet downgrades the primary outcome for exactly this ("Each study defines its own unsafe tier") and the plain language summary concedes "the worst grade in one study is not quite the same as the worst grade in another" — yet the title, abstract, Summary of Findings and Conclusions all use the unqualified word "unsafe," which asserts realised unsafety where the modal contributing tier asserts only potential unsafety. Under PROTOCOL §2.4, poolability is judged per outcome and an outcome that cannot be pooled as defined is reported as a finding, not rescued by redefinition; here the redefinition happened silently at the mapping stage, study by study, inside the team. What would fix it: (a) relabel the outcome honestly throughout — title, abstract, tables, conclusions — as the proportion placed in a study-defined unsafe *or potentially unsafe* tier, or restrict the primary pool to tiers that assert actual unsafety/harm and re-pool, reporting the potential-tier studies as a separate stratum; (b) delete or rewrite the Background sentence claiming the pooled number "means the same thing in every contributing study"; (c) add a table of the verbatim tier label and definition for every primary study (the data already exist in extraction-settled.csv) so a reader can judge the mapping; (d) disclose that Buis 2023 contributes the same 23/74 to both the unsafe and undertriage pools.

## Majors

1. **[Abstract, Main results; Summary of Findings; Table 2; Conclusions; results-summary.csv] Spin check (§2.5): the paper headlines the lowest of its own estimates and reports estimator-conditional stability as if it were stability.** The GLMM was pre-specified as primary (stage0 protocol, Analysis), so choosing it is not post-hoc cherry-picking — I concede that. But pre-specification does not license the reporting asymmetry that follows. The Hartung–Knapp sensitivity is 12.5% (11.3 to 13.9) for the main pool and 12.5, 12.4, 12.7, 12.2 and 12.5% across the four sensitivities in results-summary.csv — a *stable* estimate, two full points above the headline, whose confidence interval only just touches the GLMM interval (11.3 vs 12.0 at the join). The crude proportion, which needs no model at all, is 11.3%, also above the headline. Against that, the abstract states the pooled value "holds at 10.0% to 10.6% across four sensitivities" without saying this range is GLMM-only; a reader reasonably takes away "every reasonable analysis gives about 10.5%," when the paper's own second estimator gives about 12.5% every time. The GLMM figure is then the only number carried into the Authors' Conclusions, the Discussion's summary, the plain language summary ("about 1 in 10" — 12.5% is 1 in 8) and the practice implications ("about one time in ten"). The explanation offered for the gap — that the GLMM needs no continuity correction for zero-event arms — explains a weighting difference; it does not explain why the lower number monopolises every summary surface. What would fix it: report the headline as an estimator range (10.5% GLMM; 12.5% Hartung–Knapp; 11.3% crude) in the abstract results and conclusions, qualify the sensitivity-stability sentence as GLMM-conditional, and carry both estimates into the plain language summary and practice implications, or justify in the Methods, with a cited rationale beyond pre-specification, why the GLMM estimate should be believed over the inverse-variance estimate for *this* pool (including its handling of the 9 zero-event arms and the very large derived-count arms).

2. **[Abstract, Main results; Results, Subgroup analyses; Table 2; stage0 protocol, Subgroups; analysis-dataset.csv] The era interaction is confounded to the point of being uninterpretable, rests on a silent protocol deviation, and is over-sold in the abstract.** Four independent problems, each grounded in the package. (i) *Composition:* the "early" level is 3 arms from 2 studies — Armbruster 2024 GPT-4 (16/100) and Buis 2023 GPT-3.5 (15/37) and GPT-4 (8/37). Both studies are at **high overall risk of bias** (Table 1). Buis 2023 is the emergency-department triage set the manuscript itself singles out as the pool's extreme outlier (31.1%), so two of the three "early" arms are early *because* they are the emergency set — era and setting are perfectly entangled. Armbruster 2024's model conduct is rated High because responses were edited and a physician-role prompt was appended (Results, Risk of bias) — its 16.0% is not a clean GPT-4 measurement either. (ii) *Protocol deviation:* the stage0 protocol pre-specifies subgroup 3 as "GPT-3.5 era versus GPT-4, GPT-4o era and later." Under that definition both GPT-4 arms belong to the *late* level, the early level has a single arm, and by the analysis plan's own rule (interaction only where each level holds ≥2 units) no test exists. The manuscript instead applies a release-date cut-off (13 May 2024) across all model families, which manufactures the testable contrast. No dated amendment for this change is filed, although the protocol's Amendments section requires one before the affected analysis. (iii) *Missing arms:* 3 early + 88 late = 91, not 95; analysis-dataset.csv shows 4 arms coded era = "not_reported" (Wang Y 2026, 3 arms; An 2026, 1 arm) that vanish from the subgroup without a word in the manuscript. (iv) *Abstract billing:* the abstract reports "interaction p = 0.001" in the Main results with the same prominence as the primary outcome, while the Results and analysis-output.md correctly demote it to "a prompt for further study rather than a finding." An abstract reader — the only reader most citations ever get — receives it as a finding that newer models are safer. What would fix it: remove the era contrast from the abstract, or present it there with its basis attached ("3 arms, 2 studies, both high risk of bias"); file the dated amendment explaining the change from the protocol's generation-based subgroup to the release-date cut-off, or re-run the subgroup as protocol-specified and report that it is untestable; state the 4 era-unclassified arms in the text; and in the Results, name the confounders specifically (both studies high RoB; Buis = the emergency set; Armbruster = edited responses) in the same paragraph as the p-value, not two sections apart.

3. **[Results, Description of studies and Primary outcome; Table 1; analysis-results.json (P1_sens_no_derived); analysis-dataset.csv] Derived counts and unpoolable arms: the point estimate survives their removal, but the precision the paper sells does not — and 726 known rated responses are excluded for reporting-format reasons.** From analysis-dataset.csv: 34 of 95 primary arms (36%) are flagged derived, contributing 246 of 552 events (45%) and 2,247 of 4,868 responses (46%). The single largest primary study, Draelos 2026, enters only through derived counts (3 arms, 70/666), and its fourth arm (Gemini 1.5 Flash, 222 responses) is excluded entirely because its count exists "only as a bar in a figure." Ariel 2026 contributes no arm because per-arm counts were not printed, removing a further 504 rated responses (its printed pooled catastrophic share, 7.3%, sits *below* the headline). So 726 responses with known denominators — 13% of all rated responses the review located for the primary question (726 of 5,594) — are outside the pool for format reasons, and nearly half of what remains is reconstructed by arithmetic from rounded percentages rather than read off a page. The authors' own sensitivity is honest about the consequence and the abstract does not repeat it: excluding derived arms leaves 61 arms, the pooled value holds at 10.0%, but tau² rises from 0.30 to 0.52, I² from 42% to 49%, and the prediction interval widens from 3.8–26.2% to 2.5–32.4% (results-summary.csv). In other words, the derived arms do not drive the point estimate — I checked, and they do not — they drive the *tightness* of the confidence interval (9.2 to 12.0) that makes "one in ten" sound settled, and that dependence is invisible in the abstract and conclusions. Derived counts reconstructed from a printed percentage "within rounding" are not observations; at minimum they carry reconstruction error the GLMM treats as exact. What would fix it: state the derived share (arms, events, responses) in the abstract results and in the Primary outcome paragraph; report the no-derived prediction interval alongside the main one wherever the PI is quoted; and add a sensitivity bounding the Draelos figure-only arm and the Ariel study (e.g., Ariel at its printed 7.3% pooled share) so the reader can see what the format exclusions could do to the headline.

4. **[Results, Narrative set; Abstract; Conclusions] Selective emphasis (§2.5): 121 of 166 studies — including all the evidence about what users actually do — are summarised in one paragraph while the 23-study bench pool takes every headline.** The narrative set is 73% of the included evidence. It receives a single paragraph of unquantified impressions ("recurring findings," no counts, no table, no study list in the manuscript) and one named anomaly (Dong 2025's internal inconsistency). Yet the narrative and secondary sets contain the findings most relevant to the paper's stated question — what happens when laypeople seek this advice. The manuscript's own human-user paragraph reports Si 2026: inappropriate medication recommendations in 336 of 496 LLM encounters (67.7%) against 23.4% for primary care physicians — a harm signal six times the headline rate, on a denominator the pool's rules exclude. The narrative summary itself notes that "people often follow chatbot advice, including advice the study team judged wrong, while rating their own confidence highly," and that fabricated references "appear in studies that check citations" — the fabrication outcome the pool had to abandon (0 studies with counts) is documented behaviour in the set that gets one paragraph. A reader of the abstract learns that 121 studies exist and nothing about what they say; the pooled 10.5% from investigator-written question sets becomes the paper's message by default. That is selective reporting by emphasis, whatever the intent: the pool's eligibility line (print an explicit tier with counts, or be narrative) is also a selection mechanism correlated with how a study measured safety, and the 121 excluded-by-format studies are never characterised well enough for a reader to judge which way that selection pushes. What would fix it: a narrative-set table (or supplement referenced from the abstract) giving, per pre-specified theme, the number of studies and their direction of findings — including how many report users following advice judged wrong, how many document fabrication, and how many report *low* or no harm signals — plus one abstract sentence stating the narrative set's main convergent finding and its size relative to the pool.

5. **[Summary of Findings; Results, Secondary outcomes; analysis-output.md (S1)] The problematic-tier pool applies a poolability standard the paper itself refuses for undertriage — and one preprint supplies 61% of its denominator.** For undertriage, the authors correctly decline to summarise by the pooled mean: the pool "describes none of" its parts, PI 0.1 to 96.1%. For the problematic tier they pool anyway and headline the result in the abstract (18.7%, 17.2 to 20.2), although by their own words the tier "changes meaning between studies," the Summary of Findings concedes the figure "summarises unlike measures," I² is 96.6%, and the prediction interval is 2.8 to 64.8% — a range that makes the narrow confidence interval (17.2 to 20.2) a statement about a mean of unlike things, not about any rate a reader could encounter. The Hartung–Knapp fit for this pool, in analysis-results.json, is 19.3% with a far more honest interval (13.8 to 26.5); it appears nowhere in the manuscript. And Khan 2026 — a preprint whose classification of 5,000 responses is, per the Risk of bias section, *undescribed*, in a study at high risk of bias in the rating domain — contributes 1,191 of 2,147 events and 5,000 of 8,190 responses (61% of the denominator) to this pool. PROTOCOL §2.4: poolability is judged per outcome against the same standard. What would fix it: treat S1 as the authors treat S2 — report it study by study with the pooled mean demoted or removed from the abstract and Summary of Findings — or justify, against the undertriage precedent in the same manuscript, why this pool's mean is usable; in either case report the Hartung–Knapp interval and a sensitivity excluding Khan 2026.

6. **[Background, para 4; Discussion, Agreements and disagreements; concept-and-protocol.md; stage0 prospero-form; registry-recheck.md] The differentiation from competing record CRD420261347066 is one real point wearing five-point clothing (§2.1, contribution).** Of the five claimed differences, only one survives contact with the results: the single harm denominator. (i) "Human-user randomised and field studies eligible and analysed in their own stratum" — the stratum is empty at response level; an eligibility criterion that admitted zero analysable studies differentiates the *protocol*, not the evidence, and if Okubo and Grein report first with any human-user data at all, this claimed advantage inverts. (ii) "Primary-care presentations only" — the pool contains an emergency-department triage set (Buis 2023, retained by a coordinator scope ruling), myocardial infarction public education (Lv 2026), and psychological support for depression and anxiety (Xiao 2026a), while the scope-rulings file excludes ophthalmological emergencies as "not first-contact" — the border is real but it is not where the differentiation sentence implies, and it was drawn by the review team's own post-full-text rulings (3 out, 2 in), not by dual screening. (iii) GRADE per outcome and (iv) a CHART-informed risk-of-bias tool are methods choices, adoptable by any competing team in an afternoon; they differentiate rigour, not the question answered. (v) The comparison itself rests on a registry record with no located publication, verified only by a PubMed author search and two web searches (registry-recheck.md) — absence of evidence the manuscript elsewhere handles carefully but leans on hard here. Finally, this review is itself unregistered (no PROSPERO number issued), so the contribution claim currently amounts to: an unregistered review differentiates itself from a registered protocol it cannot show has failed to report. What would fix it: rewrite the differentiation to the one substantive point (a single pre-specified harm denominator, with counts) plus the scope point stated accurately (primary-care *and first-contact disposition* presentations, with the scope rulings acknowledged); drop the human-user eligibility point or reword it as an unmet design intention; and commit in print to re-running the competitor check on submission day, as the recheck file already recommends.

7. **[Title; Abstract; Plain language summary] The title promises a measurement of patient-facing risk that a bench-only pool cannot deliver (§2.5: a title that promises more than the study delivers is a Major).** To the authors' credit, the abstract's Main results state "The primary pool was bench-only," the Authors' Conclusions begin "In bench studies," and the plain language summary warns the results "are not a safety rating for any chatbot product." But the title — the only part of this paper most citers, journalists and policymakers will ever read — is "Unsafe medical advice from patient-facing LLM chatbots in primary-care presentations," with no bench, evaluation-set, or study-question qualifier, and the abstract's Objectives likewise omit that the estimand is performance on investigator-written or forum-lifted question sets graded afterwards. "Patient-facing" describes how the software is marketed, not how the evidence was generated: on the extraction sheet, most question sets are researcher-developed scenarios or questions "screened from" web pools and rewritten; only a minority (Armbruster 2024, Buis 2023, Draelos 2026 in part, Yu S 2026) use questions real patients actually posed, and none grades answers real patients received. The Discussion concedes the estimate "does not estimate the rate at which a patient using a chatbot tonight meets an unsafe answer." A title that needs that sentence as a correction is the wrong title. What would fix it: qualify the title and the abstract Objectives with the design — e.g., "…in bench evaluations of primary-care question sets" — so the unit of evidence travels with the claim wherever the title is quoted alone.

8. **[Declarations; Methods, Selection of studies and scope rulings; §2.6 author-position bias] Every consequential judgement call in this review was made inside a single-author team, and the declarations do not reckon with that.** The author is, by his own affiliation line, "now building clinical AI" while reviewing the safety of clinical AI chatbots, and declares "no financial or personal interest in any system evaluated." That may be literally true of the named products, but PROTOCOL §2.6 asks panels to test author-position bias harder, not softer, and the structural facts here are stark: one human author designed the protocol, and a coordinated LLM team under his direction screened, extracted, mapped each study's tier into the primary numerator (the mapping at issue in Blocker 1), applied post-full-text coordinator scope rulings that kept the pool's highest-rate study in (Buis 2023, 31.1%) while ruling other emergency sets out, chose the era cut-off that created the only significant subgroup, and settled its own disagreements — with no PROSPERO registration, no external protocol timestamp, and four records (R2022, R2073, R2966, R3051) still flagged unread for the author while the review reports itself complete. I am not alleging the calls were made in bad faith — several (excluding the high-rate ophthalmology sets, 25/50 and 19/50 harmful on the rulings file) cut *against* a larger headline. The point is that none of them is checkable against an anchor outside the team, and the manuscript's transparency about the LLM team's role, while welcome, is not a substitute for one. What would fix it: a declarations paragraph that states the author's clinical-AI building activity as a potential non-financial interest and describes which decisions were human-author decisions versus team recommendations; PROSPERO submission (or a timestamped public protocol deposit) before journal submission so the tier-mapping, scope and era decisions acquire an external reference point; and settlement or explicit sensitivity treatment of the four flagged records.

## Minors

1. **[Results, Primary outcome] The zero-event explanation undercounts the zero-event arms.** The text attributes the GLMM/Hartung–Knapp gap to "the zero-event arms (Wen 2026, 0 of 100 across five arms; Bilgic 2026, 0 of 75)." analysis-dataset.csv shows a ninth zero-event arm — Xiao 2026a, ChatGPT, 0 of 20 — for 9 zero-event arms totalling 0 of 195 responses. Correct the sentence; the point matters because the estimator gap is partly a story about how 4% of the pool's responses are handled.
2. **[Abstract, Main results; Conclusions] GRADE certainty disappears where it is most needed.** "GRADE certainty was very low for every pooled outcome" appears once, at the end of the abstract's results. The Conclusions and Implications for practice then restate "about one time in ten" with no certainty qualifier in the same sentence. Attach "very low certainty" to the restated figure in the Conclusions, as the Summary of Findings table does.
3. **[Discussion, Completeness and applicability] The completeness inference is stronger than its basis.** "Citation chasing added 80 studies and changed no primary conclusion… It suggests the primary pool is close to complete" — chasing can only find studies the included literature cites, and 138 unretrieved reports plus the unretrieved OpenAlex tail (200 of 3,962) are acknowledged two paragraphs later. Chasing finding no new primary study is equally consistent with the citing literature sharing the search's blind spots. Soften to a statement about the *cited* literature.
4. **[Results, Subgroup analyses] The red-flag "descriptive contrast" compares one study against a pool it is part of the definition of.** Buis 2023 at 31.1% "while the other 22 primary studies together sit at 11.0% crude" is a between-study contrast of the pool's known outlier against the rest, presented immediately after the (correct) statement that no red-flag subgroup test exists. Label it explicitly as a single-study observation, not a contrast, or move it to the Buis description.
5. **[Table 1] Table 1 mixes pooled and unpooled material without marking which rows enter the meta-analysis.** Ariel 2026 (4 arms, no per-arm counts) and the Draelos Gemini arm appear in the primary-pool table although neither contributes to the 95-arm pool; a reader summing the table cannot reproduce 552/4,868. Add a "contributes to pool" marker per study/arm.

## Nits

1. **[Plain language summary]** "About 1 in 10" for 10.5% is fine rounding for the GLMM; if Major 1 is adopted and the Hartung–Knapp estimate is reported alongside, "between 1 in 10 and 1 in 8" is the honest plain-language range.
2. **[Abstract]** "Model era was the only subgroup with an interaction test" is accurate but reads as a strength; "the only subgroup in which a test could be fitted" is the same fact without the varnish.
3. **[References]** Several pool-study DOIs carry 2026 dates and formats a reader cannot yet resolve against the search date (e.g., the medRxiv-style 10.64898/… identifiers). I leave resolution to the citation-integrity reviewer, but the sceptic notes the headline rests disproportionately on the newest, least-settled literature: only 2 of 23 pooled studies predate 2026.

## Closing paragraph
If this paper is published as it stands, the worst true thing a critical reader could say is this: the authors set out to stop the field pooling unlike safety grades into a meaningless number, said so in their own Background, and then pooled unlike safety grades into a number — one built mostly from tiers that assert only *potential* unsafety, nearly half of it reconstructed from rounded percentages, reported through whichever of their two estimators gave the smaller answer, and titled as though it measured what happens when patients talk to chatbots, when in fact not one of its 4,868 responses was ever received by a patient. The dataset to do better is already in their files; the paper they have written is the press release for the paper they should have written.
